Chapter 14 — Medication Information

1. Drug names, classifications and basic terminology

Generic (nonproprietary) name: standardized name of the active drug, such as morphine. Trade (proprietary) name: manufacturer's marketed name. A drug class groups medicines by their use or mechanism. Read labels carefully: similar names do not mean interchangeable effects.

Therapeutic effect is the intended benefit; a side effect is an additional, often predictable effect; an adverse reaction is a harmful or unwanted response; toxicity is harmful drug exposure; an allergic reaction is an immune-mediated response. Idiosyncratic means an unusual individual response. Do not confuse side effects with allergy.

2. Pharmacokinetics: ADME

  • Absorption: entry into circulation from an administration site. IV medication enters directly into blood, bypassing absorption from a tissue or gastrointestinal site.
  • Distribution: movement through blood to organs and tissues; influenced by circulation and tissue characteristics.
  • Metabolism: chemical transformation of drugs, often in the liver. Some oral drugs undergo first-pass metabolism before reaching systemic circulation.
  • Excretion: elimination of drug and metabolites, often through the kidneys. Reduced renal function can increase exposure to renally eliminated drugs.

Pharmacodynamics asks what the drug does to the body; pharmacokinetics asks what the body does to the drug. An agonist activates a receptor; an antagonist blocks or counteracts receptor effects. Efficacy is ability to achieve an effect; potency relates to dose required for a specified effect; a more potent drug is not necessarily more effective.

4. Recognizing urgent drug reactions

Anaphylaxis: rapidly developing breathing difficulty, swelling or cardiovascular compromise after exposure requires immediate emergency response; epinephrine is first-line treatment. Opioid toxicity: depressed respirations and reduced responsiveness require immediate help, airway/ventilation support as indicated and naloxone according to protocol. A paralytic agent does not provide pain relief, sedation or amnesia on its own.

5. Professor-style study checkpoints

Ask yourself: Is the question asking for a drug name, a drug class, an effect, or a first action? Eliminate options from the wrong category first. Watch for NOT, EXCEPT, FIRST and MOST appropriate. Distinguish naloxone from flumazenil and pharmacokinetics from pharmacodynamics.

Chapter 15 — Medication Administration

1. Medication safety and the six rights

Use the chapter's six rights: right patient, right medication, right dose, right time, right route and right documentation. Confirm patient identity with two approved identifiers, such as full name and date of birth, matched to the record and wristband. A room or bed number is not a patient identifier. Check allergies, orders, labels, expiry, administration history and institutional requirements. Do not administer a dose that appears incorrect: hold and clarify with the authorized prescriber.

Standing orders are authorized instructions that apply only when their stated conditions are met. Never treat an informal suggestion as a valid medication order. Perform procedures only within training, authorization and local policy.

Enteral refers to the GI tract; parenteral generally refers to injection routes. Enteric-coated tablets should not be crushed unless the specific formulation is verified safe to alter.

3. IV fluids, access and complications

Normal saline is 0.9% sodium chloride. Isotonic fluids have effective osmotic concentration comparable to body fluids. Peripheral IV sites may include dorsal hand veins. An antecubital catheter can kink with elbow flexion.

  • Infiltration: IV fluid enters surrounding tissue; possible signs include swelling, coolness, discomfort and reduced flow.
  • Extravasation: infused medication or fluid escapes the vessel; potentially tissue-damaging agents require urgent facility-specific management.
  • Hematoma: blood accumulates in tissue after vessel injury.
  • Priority: stop a suspected infiltrating infusion, assess and obtain qualified help per protocol. Never increase flow to overcome unexplained swelling.
  • Pump alarm: promptly check the patient, pump and tubing and obtain nursing help as appropriate.

4. Dosage and unit calculations

Weight: kilograms ≈ pounds ÷ 2.2. Example: 154 lb ÷ 2.2 = 70 kg.

Weight-based dose: ordered mg/kg × patient kg = mg. Example: 3 mg/kg × 20 kg = 60 mg.

Liquid volume: ordered mg ÷ available mg/mL = mL. Example: 15 mg ÷ 5 mg/mL = 3 mL.

Amount in syringe: mL × mg/mL = mg. Example: 2 mL × 4 mg/mL = 8 mg. 1 L = 1000 mL. Check units and independent verification requirements; a correct calculation is not authorization to give a drug.

5. Sharps and documentation

Use approved safety devices and discard used sharps immediately in the correct container. Do not recap with two hands. Document medication, dose, route, date/time, administrator and required patient response or other information under facility policy. Report and document errors according to policy.

6. Professor-style clinical priorities

When a question asks what to do FIRST, identify immediate safety concerns: wrong dose → hold and clarify; swollen IV site → stop and assess; pump alarm → check promptly; unidentified patient → verify two identifiers. For calculations, write units on each step and eliminate answers with incorrect units or magnitude.
